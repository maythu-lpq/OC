Frequently asked questions

The questions every organic chemistry class asks.

How do I decide between SN1, SN2, E1 and E2 quickly?

Look at three things in order: (1) the carbon: methyl/1° favours SN2, 3° favours SN1/E1/E2, 2° can do anything; (2) the reagent: strong base means E2, good nucleophile with weak base means SN2 (or SN1 on 3°), weak nucleophile means SN1/E1; (3) the extras: aprotic solvent helps SN2, heat helps elimination.

What is the difference between a nucleophile and a base?

Both donate an electron pair. A nucleophile attacks carbon (speed = nucleophilicity); a base takes a hydrogen (strength = basicity). I⁻, CN⁻ and N₃⁻ are good nucleophiles but weak bases. HO⁻ and EtO⁻ are both strong. t-BuOK is a strong base but too bulky to be a good nucleophile.

Why do 3° halides never do SN2?

Three alkyl groups block the back side of the carbon, so the nucleophile cannot reach it.

Why can't a 1° halide do SN1?

A primary carbocation is far too unstable to form, so ionisation never starts.

Why does a polar aprotic solvent speed up SN2?

Protic solvents (water, alcohols) hydrogen-bond to the nucleophile and calm it down. Aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, acetone) leave it free and reactive.

Why does heat favour elimination?

Elimination makes more molecules than substitution, which adds disorder (entropy). The higher the temperature, the more that matters.

Zaitsev or Hofmann?

Normal bases give the more substituted, more stable alkene (Zaitsev). A bulky base like t-BuOK takes the easiest-to-reach hydrogen and gives the less substituted alkene (Hofmann).

What is a good leaving group?

A weak base that is happy on its own: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ ≫ F⁻. HO⁻ and NH₂⁻ are poor, so alcohols must first be protonated or turned into tosylates.

What is a carbocation rearrangement?

A neighbouring H (or CH₃) slides over with its bonding pair to turn a less stable cation into a more stable one, for example 2° → 3°. It only happens in SN1/E1, never in SN2/E2.

What happens to stereochemistry?

SN2 inverts the configuration (Walden inversion). SN1 gives a mix of both configurations (racemisation). E2 needs the H and the leaving group anti to each other.

How does the rate depend on concentration?

SN1 and E1: depends only on the halide. SN2 and E2: depends on both the halide and the nucleophile/base, so doubling both makes it 4 times faster.
